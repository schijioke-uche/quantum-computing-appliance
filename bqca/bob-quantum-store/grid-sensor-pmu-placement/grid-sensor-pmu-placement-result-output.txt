EXPERIMENT INFERENCE:
WHAT WAS RUN. A one-layer QAOA circuit with 9 qubits, one per bus of the WSCC
9-bus test system, was sampled 4096 times on the real QPU ibm_marrakesh. Each
measured bitstring is a candidate PMU placement. The circuit has 120
two-qubit gates before transpilation and 151 after, with depth 320.

WHAT THE RESULT SHOWS. The best fully observable placement sampled is
{4,7,9}, using 3 PMUs, which equals the exact minimum. 2.5% of shots landed
on one of the 4 optimal placements, against 0.8% for a random guess, and
56.1% of shots observed the whole grid, against 36.1% at random.

WHY IT CAN BE TRUSTED. The answer is not taken on faith. Classical code
enumerated all 512 placements and found the exact minimum of 3 PMUs, then
re-checked that every bus is within one hop of a PMU in the reported
placement. The optimal-placement share is 3.2x the random baseline, above the
1.5x threshold used for success.

WHAT THE QPU CONTRIBUTED. It produced the samples. The noise-free prediction
for this circuit is 4.3% on optimal placements; the hardware gave 2.5%. The
gap is hardware noise, and it is the honest measure of how far today's
devices are from a clean run.

LIMITS. This is a 9-qubit workflow demonstration. It does not claim a quantum
advantage, and the result says nothing about utility-scale grids.

NOTES:
1. A PMU (phasor measurement unit) is a GPS-synchronised grid sensor. Fewer
   PMUs means lower installation and communication cost, but the operator
   must still see every bus.
2. The model is topological only: a PMU observes its own bus and its direct
   neighbours. Real planning also uses zero-injection buses, line-outage
   redundancy, channel limits and costs.
3. The QAOA angles were tuned on a noise-free classical simulation. The QPU
   only sampled the circuit; it did no learning, and its answer was checked
   against brute force.
4. This run is a 9-qubit workflow demonstration; it does not claim a quantum
   speed-up.
5. Next steps: larger IEEE test systems (14, 30, 57 buses), more QAOA layers,
   error mitigation, and zero-injection and N-1 constraints.
